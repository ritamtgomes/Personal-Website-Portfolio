Are Aviation Communications Really on .XX5 MHz? The Shift to 8.33 kHz Explained

Here's What Your Airband Radio Isn't Telling You

Rita Gomes • September 12, 2025

As an Aviation nerd and radio communications enthusiast, I always enjoyed tuning my Airband radio to my local airport (LPPT) frequencies: tower, approach, ground, you name it. However, something wasn't adding up: when ATC said something like “contact tower 118.105” or “contact approach 119.105”, I couldn't hear a thing in those frequencies. Instead, I would hear the communications loud and clear on 118.000 MHz or 119.000 MHz.

What confused me even more were pilots reporting they would hear the controller perfectly when tuning the airplane radio to the .XX5 frequencies. So how could I not hear it, even using sophisticated radios with 1 Hz precision? Initially, I suspected it was related to the 8.33 kHz spacing used in aviation bands. But even applying that spacing, my receiver remained silent on the .005 channels.

Well, the spectrum analyzer doesn't lie, and what it showed was clear: for LPPT approach, aircraft and ATC were indeed communicating on 119.100 MHz, not on the 119.105 MHz pilots were instructed to use, as seen on the picture below. This pattern was also confirmed for the LPPT tower frequency and LPFR approach and ground, frequencies that theoretically should also end in .XX5 MHz.

After doing some research on this, I discovered that the answer lies indeed in the 8.33 kHz channel spacing, just not the way I thought it would. The act of setting this spacing between frequencies on my receiver wouldn't allow me to hear on the .XX5s just by itself, simply because in reality the transmissions are not happening in those frequencies, even though pilots and air traffic controllers dial them into their radios, giving the false illusion that they are actually using it.

So here’s what really happens: The official frequency, inserted on plane’s radios, isn’t the exact frequency the aircraft is transmitting on, but a designated channel “name”. This gives us two different concepts:

  • The channel, which is the number pilots are told to dial in their radios by ATC
  • The frequency in which the communication is actually held

    • Traditional aviation radios used 25 kHz spacing, with frequencies like 118.000, 118.025, 118.050 MHz, and so on. As air traffic grew, more channels were needed, so a narrower 8.33 kHz spacing was introduced in Europe, allowing three separate channels to fit in the same space that previously held just one 25 kHz channel (since 3 × 8.33 ≈ 25) However, the displayed channel is not the literal transmitted frequency, and the aircraft radio automatically shifts the transmission to the actual frequency using appropriate RF filtering. So, in this case, the real frequency is 118.0166 and the channel used is 118.015. Another example is a true frequency of 118.025 translated to the channel 118.030.     As shown in the spectrum analyzer image previously presented, the transmitted signal only occupies about 4 kHz of bandwidth, roughly 2 kHz above and below the carrier frequency. This explains why I was unable to hear anything on frequencies like 118.105 since those 5 extra kHz were out of that range. Even with a higher bandwidth set on the radio, let’s say 10 kHz, you might hear something on 118.105, but it won’t be clear enough since widening the bandwidth increases noise and attenuation thus decreasing the quality of the received signal, making it even harder to understand what is said.

    Regarding the timeline of the 8.33 kHz spacing implementation, the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) began implementing it in the European region in 1999, and its use became required in 2007 for flights above FL195. This rule extended to all aircraft operating in EU member states, as well as Norway and Switzerland, by 2018. During that decade, airlines and air traffic controllers were given time to adapt to this narrower spacing from the older 25 kHz system.

    However, not all aircraft might be equipped with the 8.33 kHz configuration. Many planes, such as general aviation aircraft, especially those operating in non-controlled or less busy airspace, may still use the older 25 kHz system. In those cases, pilots must be aware of the official frequencies for the airfields they’re operating in, and check whether the route or airspace requires 8.33, making sure the radio can legally and safely tune to the required frequencies. A detailed document by EUROCONTROL about the 8.33 kHz spacing implementation and rules can be found here.

    As an Airclub member, this raises an important question: On airfields or airspaces where older or experimental aircraft are seen not communicating, even with a flight plan filed and all procedures correctly followed, could this spacing compatibility issue be the reason? What if those pilots were actually trying to communicate all along, but their radio equipment wasn’t aligned with this new rule, leaving air traffic controllers unable to hear them?

    In short, the real frequency is not dialed on airplanes and ATC radios. Instead, a corresponding channel is used, which the radios will then convert to the true frequency. So, if you want to listen to aviation communications using an amateur's radio, even if it comes with Airband and is set to 8.33 kHz of spacing, don't forget to translate the channels indicated by Air Traffic Controller or listed on aviation websites to their real corresponding frequencies.

    Happy communications!
